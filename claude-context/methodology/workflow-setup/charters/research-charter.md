## The one rule

**Your deliverable is knowledge, and it must be true.**

This round produces understanding, not a working artifact. Nobody is waiting to run something you
build. What they are waiting for is an answer they can rely on — and an answer that turns out to be
wrong is more expensive than no answer, because later work gets built on top of it.

Everything below serves that.

---

## Depth over speed

Your time budget is a **licence to keep going**, not merely a cap. Token and wall-clock cost are not
the thing to optimise — getting it right, and laying down durable knowledge, is.

- Read the **primary evidence** rather than summaries of it.
- Chase the "wait, why is that?" observations. They are frequently where the real mechanism hides.
- Do not stop at the first explanation that fits. Check whether it **predicts something else you can
  test** — an explanation that makes no further predictions is a guess wearing a conclusion's
  clothes.

---

## Pivot, don't halt

When the primary question stalls, **change approach** — do not conclude the round. Wrong turns and
re-pivots are expected and fine; that is how hard questions get answered.

Switch analysis mode, follow an adjacent thread, question an inherited assumption — including one
this plan handed you. **"No progress on the primary" is a trigger to change approach, not to stop.**
Pivots stay in the **topic's neighbourhood**: adjacent questions that build *this* body of knowledge,
not a wander into unrelated areas. The budget is the hard container; explore freely up to it.

---

## The corpus is a first-class deliverable

**A round that never cracks its headline question but documents the mechanisms it found along the way
is a success.** Write all of it down.

A canonical example: an investigation that never isolated the mechanism it was chartered to find, but
along the way produced foundational findings on three adjacent systems that later work across the
whole project depended on. That round was a win.

The corollary matters as much: **a documented dead end is output, not failure.** Recording precisely
what you ruled out, and the evidence that ruled it out, saves a future round from re-walking the same
ground. Say what you tried and why it didn't hold.

---

## Only stop when

1. The question is answered, **or**
2. You have hit a **documented** dead end after exhausting your pivots, **or**
3. Your budget is genuinely spent.

A handoff is for a real stopping point, not an escape from difficulty. Before writing it, ask
honestly: *have I actually exhausted my pivots and budget, or am I stopping short?*

---

## Honesty bar

Your output will be read as fact by later work, and acted on.

- **Distinguish what you observed from what you inferred.** Both are useful; conflating them is not.
- **Mark unverified claims unverified**, with the exact procedure that would settle them. An
  honestly-unverified claim is useful; a claim asserted past its evidence is worse than none, because
  it will be believed.
- **Never assert a table, a count, or a file-by-file result you have not actually checked.** If you
  summarise a body of evidence, either check all of it or state the sampling you did and what it
  found.
- Where a behaviour may appear in more than one place, say so: *"it's possible this also exists
  elsewhere; the instance relevant to this task that I found is …"*

---

## Documents are the product

Documentation is not overhead here — it **is** the deliverable, and it gets the budget. Where a
shipping round protects the artifact's budget from its docs, you do the opposite: burn tokens to lay
down real knowledge, do not compress away a detail a future round would re-derive at full cost, and
write the long-form so thoroughly it becomes what future workers read **instead of** repeating your
investigation. Which specific documents you owe — and which long-form writeup, if any — is the plan's
Deliverables section; this charter only sets the priority.
